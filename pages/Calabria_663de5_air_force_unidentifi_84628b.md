## Introduction

An Italian Air Force record marked “unidentified” does not confirm an alien craft, advanced technology or even a solid physical object. It means that, after the checks possible from the information supplied, officials did not establish a satisfactory technical or natural explanation. Sometimes the remaining mystery is genuinely interesting. In other cases, it chiefly reflects missing data: no photograph, no radar return, uncertain distance, an imprecise time or a witness description too brief to test.

That distinction is central to [Calabria]({{ 'why-calabria-has-many-ufo-reports-but-no/' | relative_url }})’s official UFO history. Several Calabrian reports appear in the Air Force archive with an unidentified ruling, including observations made by Carabinieri personnel and a civil aircraft crew. Their official status matters because they entered a national-security reporting system rather than only a private UFO catalogue. Yet the published summaries rarely contain enough detail to show that the reported phenomena were extraordinary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/ovni/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aeronautica Militare OVNI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Titolare del potere sostitutivo per l&#x27;area tecnico-operativa del Ministero della Difesa Sostegno alla ricollocazione Disposizioni applica...</span></span></span>

## Why the Air Force receives UFO notifications

The Italian Air Force’s role grew out of the large national wave of sightings in 1978. Following that surge, Prime Minister Giulio Andreotti designated the Air Force as the state body responsible for collecting, verifying and monitoring reports of unidentified flying objects. The work is now handled by the General Security Department of the Air Staff. Its stated purpose is flight safety and national security, not the search for extraterrestrial life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/en/category/ovni/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica MilitareOVNI ArchivesOggetti Volanti Non Identificati A seguito dell&#x27;ondata di avvistamenti di Oggetti Volanti Non Identifica...</span></span></span>

That institutional purpose explains why reports can be relevant even when their eventual cause is mundane. An unknown light near an airway might be an unreported aircraft, a balloon, a meteor, a satellite, a drone or an astronomical object. Until it is identified, officials may still need to ask whether it could interfere with aviation, indicate unauthorised activity or reveal a gap in situational awareness.

Brigadier General Massimo Berti, then head of the department, described the Air Force’s task in similarly practical terms in 2015. He said reported events were examined to determine whether they might represent a threat, especially to flight safety. He also stressed that deciding whether intelligent life exists elsewhere was not an Air Force responsibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.media.inaf.it/2015/09/14/massimo-berti-intervista/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.inaf.it">[MEDIA INAF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.inaf.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">massimo berti intervista</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">L’Aeronautica Militare indaga sempre su questi episodi, allo scopo di stabilire se essi possano costituire una minaccia di qualsiasi tipo...</span></span></span>

The military connection therefore should not be exaggerated. The Air Force receives reports because unusual aerial activity falls naturally within aviation and security responsibilities. Its involvement does not mean that every file was treated as evidence of a secret aircraft or subjected to a full scientific investigation.

## How a report enters the archive

Members of the public can submit an observation using the Air Force’s five-page reporting form. The witness is instructed to complete only the details remembered with certainty and deliver the signed document to a Carabinieri station, which forwards it to the Air Staff. Photographs, video or other supporting material may be attached.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/ModuloUFO-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

The form asks for much more than a simple description of a “flying saucer”. It seeks the date and precise local time, weather conditions, the observer’s position and whether the object was seen through glass, spectacles, binoculars or another instrument. Later questions cover apparent shape, colour, angular size, direction, movement, duration, sound and the presence of other witnesses. This structure is designed to reduce ambiguity and make comparisons with known aerial or astronomical phenomena possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/ModuloUFO-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

Signing and filing the form also gives the report a degree of administrative accountability. Berti characterised submission through the Carabinieri as a formal act through which the citizen assumes responsibility for the account and the Air Force undertakes to process it. That improves traceability, but it does not automatically verify the witness’s distance estimates, interpretation or memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.media.inaf.it/2015/09/14/massimo-berti-intervista/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.inaf.it">[MEDIA INAF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.inaf.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">massimo berti intervista</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">L’Aeronautica Militare indaga sempre su questi episodi, allo scopo di stabilire se essi possano costituire una minaccia di qualsiasi tipo...</span></span></span>

After checks are completed, the Air Force publishes brief annual or historical summaries. Its public explanation states that a case is classified as an unidentified flying-object sighting when no technical or natural justification can be found. The surviving tables commonly list the location, time, apparent form and colour, estimated motion and height, weather, source of the report and a short finding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/en/2023/01/12/ovni/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aeronautica Militare OVNI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica MilitareOVNI - Aeronautica Militare12 Jan 2023 — Oggetti Volanti Non Identificati · Modulo Avvistamento OVNI · 2022 · 2021 ·...</span></span></span>

What is usually absent from the public table is equally important. Readers may not receive the complete witness statement, interview notes, astronomical calculations, air-traffic information, radar data or a detailed account of which explanations were considered. The archive is consequently valuable as an official index, but often inadequate for independently reproducing the ruling.

## What the Calabrian rulings contain

The December 1978 entries show both the value and the limits of the system. At San Tifeo in the province of Cosenza, Carabinieri personnel reported a low, circular red phenomenon at about 3 a.m. on 12 December, moving from north to south. The archive’s finding says that, on the basis of the data examined, the event was catalogued as an unidentified flying object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Archivio_OVNI_periodo1972-1990.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

Two days later, at Corigliano Calabro, another report from Carabinieri personnel described a white phenomenon at about 7.15 a.m. Its form and movement were recorded as variable, its speed as low, and the sky as clear. Those official entries establish several facts: reports were made by law-enforcement personnel; dates and basic observational details were preserved; and no explanation was entered in the published outcome. They do not establish measured dimensions, exceptional acceleration, radar confirmation or physical effects. Even the descriptions “low”, “slow” and “circular” appear to be visual estimates rather than instrument readings.

The archive also records an observation about 30 nautical miles south-east of Reggio Calabria on 12 January 1989. A civil aircraft crew reported a luminous trail at roughly 8,000 metres, apparently falling towards the sea in clear conditions. The entry was retained as unidentified. A second civil crew reported a luminous trail at the same time south of Ponza, although the published summaries do not make clear whether the two reports concerned the same event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Archivio_OVNI_periodo1972-1990.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

That 1989 report has a stronger aviation connection than most public sightings because the witness source was an aircraft crew and an approximate altitude was recorded. Nevertheless, the summary lacks the information needed to distinguish among a meteor, re-entering space debris, an unusual atmospheric event or another transient phenomenon. Its unresolved status is therefore a legitimate finding about the available file, not a positive identification of an extraordinary vehicle.

A further Corigliano Calabro entry dated 30 April 1985 describes a matt-black spherical object, apparently travelling south at high speed and an estimated altitude of about 800 metres. It was reported by Carabinieri personnel and also catalogued as unidentified. Once again, however, the public record supplies no radar corroboration, duration, angular measurement or detailed investigation narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Archivio_OVNI_periodo1972-1990.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

## Why credible witnesses do not settle a case

Reports from police officers, military personnel or pilots deserve attention because such witnesses may be accustomed to observing aircraft, judging direction or reporting events carefully. Their occupations can also make fabrication less likely when the account is formally submitted.

Professional standing is not the same as infallible perception. Pilots and officers can still misjudge the size, altitude or speed of a distant light, especially at night or without a fixed background. A point source may appear to move because of observer motion; an astronomical object can seem unusually bright in clear air; and a meteor’s path may be interpreted as much lower or nearer than it really is.

The Calabrian archive therefore supports a measured conclusion. Some reports came from witnesses whose testimony carries more weight than an anonymous rumour. That raises their evidential value above a vague press anecdote. It does not remove the need for independent sensor data, accurate timing, multiple viewing positions or a documented chain of analysis.

Witness quality and case quality must be assessed separately. A sincere and capable observer may accurately report seeing something puzzling while the surviving evidence remains too poor to determine what caused it.

## The gap between unresolved and extraordinary

An unresolved ruling answers a narrow question: could officials identify the reported phenomenon from the information available? An extraordinary claim asks a much larger one: does the evidence demonstrate an object with capabilities or origins outside ordinary experience?

Moving from the first conclusion to the second requires additional evidence. A strong case would normally need several of the following:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* independent witnesses viewing from meaningfully different positions;
* precise times and directions that permit astronomical or flight checks;
* original photographs or video with reliable metadata;
* radar, infrared or other instrument confirmation;
* consistent estimates of distance, altitude and speed;
* evidence that conventional aircraft, balloons, meteors, satellites and atmospheric effects were specifically excluded;
* records detailed enough for independent analysts to repeat the assessment.

</div>

The published Calabrian entries generally do not meet that standard. They preserve concise descriptions and an administrative conclusion, but not the full body of evidence behind it. This does not prove that the observations had ordinary causes. It means that the archive cannot, by itself, support a more ambitious interpretation.

Comparable official archives illustrate the same semantic problem. Britain’s National Archives explains that, in military usage, a UFO is something seen in the sky but not recognised, rather than something extraterrestrial. The United States National Archives similarly notes that “unidentified” does not mean alien or impossible. ## Why cases can remain unidentified

A case may stay open because the phenomenon was genuinely unusual, but several less dramatic failure points are common.

**The report arrived too late.** Air-traffic records, temporary aviation notices or local launch information may no longer be readily available.

**The witness supplied estimates rather than measurements.** Apparent altitude and speed are extremely difficult to judge without knowing an object’s true size or distance.

**The observation was too brief.** A meteor lasting seconds may be vividly remembered but leave little information for analysis.

**No independent record survived.** Without imagery, radar or another witness, investigators may have only a narrative account.

**Several ordinary explanations remain possible.** A file can be unidentified because no single option can be proved, even though none requires exotic technology.

The reporting form tries to limit these weaknesses by asking detailed questions, but a carefully designed questionnaire cannot recover information that the observer never perceived. Modern research on unidentified aerial reports likewise stresses that data quality, preservation and curation are essential; a large collection of poorly constrained observations does not automatically become strong scientific evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.15368" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

## What the archive can and cannot tell us

For Calabria, the archive’s greatest value is historical and institutional. It confirms that unusual aerial reports from the region reached a formal national channel, that some were made by Carabinieri personnel or aircrew, and that officials did not assign an explanation in the summaries released to the public. It also places the December 1978 reports within the wider reporting wave that led Italy to formalise the Air Force’s role.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/en/category/ovni/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica MilitareOVNI ArchivesOggetti Volanti Non Identificati A seguito dell&#x27;ondata di avvistamenti di Oggetti Volanti Non Identifica...</span></span></span>

The records are less useful as proof of extraordinary flight. Their standardised tables compress complicated observations into a few fields, and the repeated formula stating that an event was catalogued as unidentified reveals little about the depth of the checks. “Official” describes the custodian and administrative process; it does not guarantee that the underlying evidence was complete.

The fairest reading is therefore neither dismissive nor sensational. The Calabrian rulings should not be rewritten as confirmed spacecraft encounters, but nor should they be treated as if no one reported anything. They are documented observations whose causes were not established from the files available to the Air Force. That unresolved remainder is real, but its meaning is limited: it marks the boundary of the investigation, not the discovery of an exotic answer.
