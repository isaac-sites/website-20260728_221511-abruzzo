## Introduction

Much of Umbria’s UFO history exists today not because public authorities maintained a complete regional record, but because civilian researchers saved material that might otherwise have disappeared. They clipped reports from local newspapers, contacted witnesses, compared retellings, recorded possible explanations and placed each report within a regional catalogue. This work turned isolated stories from [Nocera Umbra]({{ 'nocera-1977/' | relative_url }}), Terni, Polino and [Orvieto]({{ 'orvieto/' | relative_url }}) into a traceable—though still incomplete—history.

The central organisation was the Centro Italiano Studi Ufologici (CISU), a private research association whose regional catalogue project began in 1981. Its method matters as much as its conclusions. An archived case is evidence that a report was made and documented; it is not proof that an extraordinary object was present. In [Umbria]({{ 'why-umbrias-ufo-stories-still-resist/' | relative_url }}), civilian archives have therefore performed two roles at once: preserving forgotten testimony and exposing how uncertain, duplicated or explainable many UFO reports can be.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cisu.org/ufo-in-italia/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cisu.org">[CISU]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cisu.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ufo in Italia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ufo in Italia - CISU - Centro Italiano Studi UfologiciMay 20, 2016 — Dopo oltre 10 anni di lavoro di raccolta e catalogazione di dati...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2016</span></span></span>

## The regional catalogue project

CISU’s Regional Catalogues Project was created to build local case archives maintained by regional or provincial researchers. Information gathered by this network fed into a national catalogue, which already contained more than 8,600 Italian reports when its first major version was presented in 1991. The underlying files were not merely lists of dates and places. They could include newspaper articles, witness statements, correspondence and investigators’ assessments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cisu.org/ufo-in-italia/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cisu.org">[CISU]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cisu.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ufo in Italia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ufo in Italia - CISU - Centro Italiano Studi UfologiciMay 20, 2016 — Dopo oltre 10 anni di lavoro di raccolta e catalogazione di dati...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2016</span></span></span>

That structure was particularly valuable in Umbria. The region lacked a continuous official UFO chronology, and many early incidents survived chiefly through brief local reporting. A story printed once in a provincial edition might never appear in a national newspaper, aviation document or police archive. By locating the cutting, identifying the alleged witness and assigning the event a date and place, civilian cataloguers gave later researchers a starting point that otherwise might not exist.

The project also made comparison possible. Once reports were organised chronologically, researchers could ask whether several observations belonged to the same episode, whether a supposed regional “wave” was concentrated in a few days, or whether later accounts had drifted from the earliest wording. Such comparisons are difficult when cases circulate only as anecdotes detached from their original publication dates.

Civilian cataloguing was not unique to Umbria. CISU’s wider system used local curators across Italy, while its central archive accumulated tens of thousands of newspaper articles and case files. Recent regional catalogues elsewhere show the intended level of detail: a case may be accompanied by its documentary sources, an assessment of witness reliability and strangeness, and any proposed identification. These examples help explain the framework within which Umbrian material was preserved, even though an equally detailed public catalogue devoted solely to Umbria has not been released.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cisu.org/ufo-in-calabria-il-catalogo-dei-casi/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cisu.org">[CISU]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cisu.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO in Calabria: il catalogo dei casi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO in Calabria: il catalogo dei casi - CISU - Centro Italiano Studi UfologiciSeptember 13, 2023 — E’ fresca di stampa presso le Ediz...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 13, 2023</span></span></span>

## Newspapers created the first record

Local newspapers were often the first—and sometimes the only—record of an Umbrian sighting. Their importance is clearest in the 1977 Nocera Umbra encounter, in which a railway worker reportedly saw a bright, oddly shaped figure or object moving along a road in front of his car. The case was revived decades later when CISU researchers discussed it at a national conference in Terni, referring back to contemporary newspaper coverage rather than to surviving physical evidence or an official investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tuttoggi.info/ufo-in-umbria-4-avvistamenti-in-30-anni-i-lavori-del-25-convegno-nazionale-ufologia-terni-video-e-foto-tuttoggi-info/85047/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tuttoggi.info">[Tuttoggi.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tuttoggi.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UF O IN UMBRIA, 4 AVVISTAMENTI IN 30 ANNI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">I LAVORI...November 14, 2010 — 14 Nov 2010 — “Seguendo un criterio cronologico il primo avvistamento è avvenuto nel 1977 a Nocera Umbra...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2010</span></span></span>

That dependence on press material gives the story historical visibility, but it also limits what can confidently be said. A newspaper report may compress an interview, omit questions asked by the journalist or favour the most dramatic details. Unless the original witness statement and investigative notes are available, later readers cannot easily determine which features came directly from the witness and which were sharpened during publication or [retelling]({{ 'retelling/' | relative_url }}).

The problem becomes greater when an old article is quoted by a newer article rather than examined directly. Each stage can introduce small alterations in the object’s shape, distance, movement or duration. An archive helps by preserving the earliest accessible version, but it cannot restore information that the original reporter never recorded.

CISU’s national press archive illustrates the scale of the preservation task. During Italy’s major 1978 UFO wave, its researchers collected more than 2,000 newspaper cuttings in a single year. The surviving material filled several archive boxes and was later scanned and indexed by publication and date. CISU describes the 1978 wave as partly a media wave, an observation that is directly relevant to Umbria: newspaper coverage did not simply document sightings but also influenced how rapidly similar reports were noticed, described and reported.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cisu.org/progetto1978/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cisu.org">[CISU]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cisu.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cisu.org.</span></span></span>

## Interviews and investigator files added missing detail

Newspaper cuttings supplied leads, not finished investigations. Civilian researchers could then approach witnesses, reconstruct viewing conditions and look for conventional objects that matched the description. The resulting case file might contain sketches, questionnaires, maps, notes about weather or astronomy, and correspondence produced long after the original event.

The Umbrian cases presented at the 2010 CISU conference show the value of this second layer. Researchers Massimo Valloscuro and Andrea Bovo assembled documentation on four selected regional incidents rather than merely repeating every reported light as an unsolved mystery. One case was compared with a commercially available solar balloon or inflatable toy and classified as a “non-UFO”, while observations at [Polino]({{ 'polino/' | relative_url }}) in 2001 and near Orvieto in 2010 were described at the time as unresolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tuttoggi.info/ufo-in-umbria-4-avvistamenti-in-30-anni-i-lavori-del-25-convegno-nazionale-ufologia-terni-video-e-foto-tuttoggi-info/85047/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tuttoggi.info">[Tuttoggi.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tuttoggi.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UF O IN UMBRIA, 4 AVVISTAMENTI IN 30 ANNI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">I LAVORI...November 14, 2010 — 14 Nov 2010 — “Seguendo un criterio cronologico il primo avvistamento è avvenuto nel 1977 a Nocera Umbra...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2010</span></span></span>

This distinction reveals what an investigator’s file can add. A press story may end with the object “disappearing mysteriously”. A case review can ask whether its appearance, direction and motion resemble a balloon, aircraft, astronomical body or other known source. Even when no identification is reached, the file may show whether the failure resulted from genuinely unusual reported behaviour or simply from missing information.

Local investigators also provided continuity. CISU’s territorial network assigned representatives to conduct inquiries and compile provincial or regional catalogues. Its published directory has included an Orvieto-based representative, indicating that reports from southern Umbria could be handled by someone familiar with the area rather than being assessed entirely from a distant national office.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cisu.org/chi-siamo/rappresentanti-locali-del-cisu/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cisu.org">[CISU]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cisu.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rappresentanti locali</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gian Paolo Grassino – viale Umbria, 10 – 10034 Orbassano (TO).... Gian Pietro Donati – via Battista Gambaro, 63 – 28068 Romentino (NO)...</span></span></span>

Local familiarity can improve an investigation: a researcher may recognise a flight path, a hill that blocks part of the horizon or a festival that released illuminated balloons. Yet it does not guarantee impartiality or technical completeness. Civilian investigators vary in experience, and many cases still lack instrument data, exact timings or independent witnesses. Their files are best understood as research records whose quality must be judged individually.

## Why archive totals can mislead

Civilian catalogues make counting possible, but the resulting totals are easy to misunderstand. One Umbrian article reported 40 regional notifications in 2010 and 21 in 2011, whereas the 2010 conference presentation highlighted only four notable incidents over roughly three decades. The figures are not necessarily incompatible. They may represent different categories: all incoming notifications, documented reports, investigated cases, selected conference examples or incidents still unresolved after review.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.realumbria.it/21/04/2021/oggetti-volanti-non-identificati-umbria/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: realumbria.it">[Real Umbria]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">realumbria.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on realumbria.it.</span></span></span>

A raw catalogue entry may also represent very little evidence. It could originate from a short newspaper item, an anonymous message, a photograph with no reliable chain of custody or several witnesses describing the same object separately. Without knowing how duplicates, solved cases and weak reports were treated, regional totals cannot be read as a count of unexplained craft.

Modern catalogue design makes these distinctions more visible. CISU’s newer public catalogue work can attach sources, locations and proposed explanations to individual entries rather than presenting a single headline number. Its national CisuCat database was announced with approximately 43,000 Italian sighting reports and related notices, a total that reflects decades of collection rather than 43,000 strong unresolved cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cisu.org/cisucat2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cisu.org">[CISU]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cisu.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cat: il catalogo di segnalazioni italiane di avvistamenti UFO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cat: il catalogo di segnalazioni italiane di avvistamenti UFO</span></span></span>

For Umbria, the absence of a fully public, case-by-case regional dataset remains an important limitation. Readers often encounter totals through secondary news articles without being able to inspect the underlying files. A more transparent catalogue would distinguish at least four categories: identified events, probably identified events, reports lacking enough information, and genuinely unresolved cases with comparatively good documentation.

## Preservation did not equal confirmation

The most important lesson from Umbria’s civilian archives is that preservation and validation are different tasks. Saving a newspaper cutting confirms that an article appeared. Recording an interview confirms that a witness gave a particular account. Neither confirms that the perception was accurate or that the object had extraordinary properties.

This matters because catalogues naturally give even weak reports a durable form. Once an event has a case number, date and location, it may appear more authoritative than it was at the time. Later writers can cite the catalogue while overlooking whether the original source was anonymous, whether the observation lasted only seconds or whether investigators proposed an ordinary explanation.

CISU’s stated archival model is nevertheless more cautious than simple mystery collecting. Its holdings include investigation reports, press coverage, specialist publications and scientific material, and the organisation presents the archive as a resource for rational research. Regional catalogue publications elsewhere have explicitly included assessments of reliability, strangeness and possible identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cisu.org/chi-siamo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cisu.org">[CISU]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cisu.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Chi siamo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">è nato alla fine nel 1985 ereditando l’esperienza maturata precedentemente in altre realtà associative, per dare un nuovo e più moderno i...</span></span></span>

The solved Umbrian example involving a probable solar toy demonstrates why this matters. An archive that retained only “strange object seen in Umbria” would help perpetuate a mystery. A file that also preserved the later comparison and changed classification records the history of the investigation, including the weakening of the original claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tuttoggi.info/ufo-in-umbria-4-avvistamenti-in-30-anni-i-lavori-del-25-convegno-nazionale-ufologia-terni-video-e-foto-tuttoggi-info/85047/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tuttoggi.info">[Tuttoggi.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tuttoggi.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UF O IN UMBRIA, 4 AVVISTAMENTI IN 30 ANNI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">I LAVORI...November 14, 2010 — 14 Nov 2010 — “Seguendo un criterio cronologico il primo avvistamento è avvenuto nel 1977 a Nocera Umbra...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2010</span></span></span>

Conversely, “unresolved” should not be treated as a positive conclusion. It often means that researchers lacked enough reliable data to choose among several possibilities. The Polino and Orvieto reports may remain interesting parts of the regional record, but their archival status does not establish advanced technology, non-human origin or even a solidly documented structured object.

## An archive-shaped regional history

Civilian researchers did more than save individual stories; they determined which Umbrian incidents became part of the region’s recognised UFO history. Cases with retrievable articles, reachable witnesses or detailed files were more likely to be discussed at conferences and repeated in later journalism. Reports that left no cutting, name or usable date could disappear regardless of how striking the original experience seemed.

This creates a form of archival selection. Umbria’s apparent chronology reflects not only what people saw but what newspapers chose to print, what collectors managed to locate and what investigators considered worth pursuing. The archive is therefore a map of surviving documentation, not a complete map of unusual events.

It also means that later reporting can either strengthen or weaken an incident. Finding an original article may establish that a story was not recently invented. A witness interview may clarify the sequence of events. A comparison with a balloon or aircraft may remove the case from the unexplained category. Alternatively, discovering that all modern accounts descend from one short newspaper paragraph may reveal that the evidential foundation is much thinner than the story’s longevity suggests.

For readers of Umbria’s UFO history, civilian archives are consequently the best route into the subject and one of the main reasons for caution. They preserve regional memory, expose forgotten sources and allow cases to be checked against one another. At the same time, their gaps, uneven files and changing classifications show why a documented UFO report is not the same thing as a confirmed anomaly.
