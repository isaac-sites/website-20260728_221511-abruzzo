## Introduction

CISU’s interactive map turns decades of Apulian UFO reporting into a place-based archive. A reader can search a town, open a marker and see a short account of what was reported, when and where it happened, which documentary source preserved it and, in some entries, whether investigators suggested an ordinary explanation. Photographs or witness drawings may also be attached. The project is based on the regional catalogue assembled by Arcangelo “Lello” Cassano over nearly forty years and published in book form in 2022. CISU says that almost 1,000 Apulian cases have already been geolocated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cisu.org/ufo-in-puglia-geolocalizzazione-degli-avvistamenti/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cisu.org">[CISU - Centro Italiano Studi Ufologici]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cisu.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Centro Italiano Studi UfologiciUFO in Puglia: geolocalizzazione degli avvistamenti - CISU - Centro Italiano Studi Ufologici...</span></span></span>

Its real value is archival, not sensational. The map makes it easier to find reports from Bari, Foggia, Lecce, Taranto and smaller communities that would otherwise remain scattered through newspapers, correspondence and investigation files. But every marker represents a recorded claim or associated document, not proof that an extraordinary object was present. The map shows where stories entered the record; it cannot, by location alone, establish what caused them.

## How the geolocation archive is organised

CISU presents the material through a collection of Google My Maps rather than one undifferentiated regional display. For reports from 1950 to 1999, cases are divided by decade and can be viewed either by province or by observation type. Later periods are split into shorter date ranges because the number of collected cases became too large for the earlier decade-by-decade arrangement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cisu.org/ufo-in-puglia-geolocalizzazione-degli-avvistamenti/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cisu.org">[CISU - Centro Italiano Studi Ufologici]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cisu.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Centro Italiano Studi UfologiciUFO in Puglia: geolocalizzazione degli avvistamenti - CISU - Centro Italiano Studi Ufologici...</span></span></span>

## What each mapped entry may contain

At its best, an entry functions as a compact archival index. CISU says users may find the date and place of the event, a summary of the observation, documentary references, a possible identification and, in some cases, a photograph or witness sketch. These fields allow a reader to distinguish between a bare anecdote and a report supported by contemporary material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cisu.org/ufo-in-puglia-geolocalizzazione-degli-avvistamenti/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cisu.org">[CISU - Centro Italiano Studi Ufologici]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cisu.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Centro Italiano Studi UfologiciUFO in Puglia: geolocalizzazione degli avvistamenti - CISU - Centro Italiano Studi Ufologici...</span></span></span>

The source field is particularly important. Apulian cases have entered UFO catalogues through different routes: direct witness interviews, local newspaper reports, correspondence, photographs, later recollections and investigations conducted by civilian researchers. These sources are not equally strong. A contemporary signed statement containing precise direction, duration and weather details is potentially more useful than a brief newspaper paragraph written years after the event. A photograph with an original file and known camera settings offers more scope for examination than a compressed image copied from social media.

An identification hypothesis also changes how a marker should be read. An entry may remain in the catalogue even when investigators regard a conventional explanation as likely. This is sensible archival practice: removing explained cases would conceal how ordinary phenomena become UFO reports and would make it harder to study recurring causes. A catalogue of reports is therefore broader than a list of unresolved incidents.

Cassano’s role helps explain the map’s depth. He began investigating sightings with a Bari research group in the 1970s, joined CISU in 1986 and later managed regional archives for [Apulia]({{ 'why-apulias-ufo-record-still-resists/' | relative_url }}) and Basilicata. He produced earlier editions of an Apulian catalogue in the 1990s, maintained a regional digital archive between 1999 and 2021 and expanded the work into the 2022 book *UFO sulla Puglia*. The map is the public-facing continuation of that long cataloguing effort rather than a newly assembled collection of internet stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cisu.org/arcangelo-cassano/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cisu.org">[CISU - Centro Italiano Studi Ufologici]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cisu.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Centro Italiano Studi Ufologici Arcangelo Cassano</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Centro Italiano Studi UfologiciArcangelo Cassano - CISU - Centro Italiano Studi Ufologici...</span></span></span>

That history is also a reason to examine entries critically. A database built over several decades may combine records created under different standards. Older cases might survive only through press cuttings or retrospective testimony, while recent entries may include digital images, precise timestamps or rapid follow-up interviews. Two markers on the same map can therefore have very different evidential weight.

## Patterns the map can reveal

The clearest contribution of geolocation is that it makes patterns easier to notice. A conventional chronological catalogue may show that many reports occurred in a particular year, but a map can reveal whether they were dispersed across Apulia or concentrated around a few towns. It can also help readers compare coastal reports with inland ones, find repeated observations from the same locality or follow the geographical spread of a regional sighting wave.

This is useful for investigating several kinds of question:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Did nearby towns report similar phenomena on the same evening?
* Was an apparent cluster confined to one newspaper’s circulation area?
* Do repeated reports come from a heavily populated city rather than an isolated hotspot?
* Are coastal cases associated with observations over the Adriatic or Ionian Sea?
* Does a peak coincide with a well-known national UFO wave, a meteor shower, a satellite event or the growing popularity of sky lanterns?
* Are several markers independent reports, or multiple articles referring to one incident?

</div>

The last question matters because maps can visually exaggerate the amount of evidence. Five markers around Bari may represent five separate observations, but they could also arise from one event reported by several witnesses, repeated newspaper coverage or duplicate entries drawn from different sources. Establishing independence requires reading the individual records, not merely counting dots.

The map is also suited to historical comparison. CISU’s broader regional-catalogue project began in 1981 and was designed to preserve local case material before it disappeared. Bringing the Apulian catalogue online allows older newspaper-era reports to be searched alongside cases from the digital period. That continuity can show how the language, volume and documentation of reports changed as cameras, satellites, social media and public awareness changed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cisu.org/ufo-in-italia/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cisu.org">[CISU - Centro Italiano Studi Ufologici]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cisu.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Centro Italiano Studi Ufologici Ufo in Italia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Centro Italiano Studi UfologiciUfo in Italia - CISU - Centro Italiano Studi UfologiciIniziato infatti nel 1981, il “Progetto Catal...</span></span></span>

## Why location does not prove explanation

A cluster on a map is a starting point for investigation, not evidence of a physical concentration of unknown craft. Report totals are shaped by where people live, how often they watch the sky, whether local media publicise sightings and whether witnesses know where to submit them.

Research using much larger public UFO-report datasets has found that human and environmental factors strongly influence mapped patterns. Population, visibility, seasonal conditions and access to the night sky can all affect the number of reports. One US study found relationships between public sightings and population, light pollution, tree cover and proximity to airports or military areas. Another analysis found a seasonal pattern, with more reports during periods offering better weather for outdoor observation. These studies do not directly explain the Apulian catalogue, but they show why dots cannot be interpreted without accounting for opportunities to observe and report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-024-04182-z" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Looking up the sky: unidentified aerial phenomena and...by N Goldstein · 2024 · Cited by 1 — Figure 1 shows the monthly number of...</span></span></span>

A city may appear prominent simply because it contains more potential witnesses and more journalists. A coastal area may generate more entries because residents and visitors have long, unobstructed views of the horizon. Reports near an airport may involve aircraft seen under unusual lighting or approach angles. A military facility can attract attention because unusual aviation activity occurs nearby, but it is also an area where more observers may deliberately watch the sky. None of these factors automatically explains a particular case; they warn against treating geographical correlation as causation.

Reporting culture creates another layer of bias. A well-publicised sighting can prompt other people to reconsider ordinary lights they might previously have ignored. Researchers studying historical UFO-report data have treated such collections partly as records of human reporting behaviour, because publicity, expectation and social attention can affect when people decide that an observation is worth recording.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378437122005295" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

The absence of markers is equally ambiguous. It may mean that few unusual observations occurred, but it may also reflect sparse population, poor newspaper preservation, reluctance to report or gaps in the researcher’s sources. The map documents what reached the archive, not every unusual thing ever seen over Apulia.

## A civilian archive, not an official finding

CISU is a private research association, and its regional map should not be confused with the Italian Air Force’s official reporting process. CISU’s catalogue is historical and inclusive, drawing together reports and documents for research. The Air Force’s system has a narrower institutional purpose connected to flight and national security. Reports submitted through the official route are passed via the Carabinieri for technical checks against human activity and natural phenomena; only cases without an identified technical or natural explanation are classified officially as unidentified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/ovni/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aeronautica Militare OVNI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare OVNI</span></span></span>

The two collections therefore answer different questions. CISU’s map asks, in effect, “What UFO-related reports have been recorded in this place?” The Air Force record asks, “Which reports entered the official system, were investigated under its procedure and remained without an established explanation?” A town can have several CISU markers and no corresponding official case, without either archive being wrong.

Nor does the term “unidentified” carry the same weight in every entry. It may indicate that investigators exhausted plausible explanations, but it can also mean that a report lacked enough information to decide. A light seen for seconds with no reliable distance, direction or angular size may remain unidentified because the evidence is poor. That is very different from an observation supported by multiple independent witnesses, radar data and calibrated imagery.

## The map’s present limits

CISU announced the Apulian geolocation project in February 2026 as the first example of an entire Italian regional case collection being placed online in this form. The announcement states that almost 1,000 cases were already searchable and describes the catalogue as the result of nearly forty years of work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cisu.org/geo-localizzazione-degli-avvistamenti-lesempio-della-puglia/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cisu.org">[CISU - Centro Italiano Studi Ufologici]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cisu.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Centro Italiano Studi UfologiciGeo-localizzazione degli avvistamenti: l&#x27;esempio della Puglia - CISU - Centro Italiano Studi Ufologici...</span></span></span>

The public map also does not provide, on its main page, a detailed explanation of duplicate control, coordinate precision, confidence grading or the exact criteria used to decide whether a source becomes a separate case. Those details would be necessary for formal statistical work. A marker positioned at a town centre may indicate only the named municipality, not the precise observation point. Counting markers without knowing these rules could produce misleading hotspot claims.

## What the map ultimately shows

CISU’s project makes Apulia’s UFO history easier to examine at street, town and provincial scale. It can reveal where reports accumulated, help recover overlooked local cases and connect brief sightings with the documents from which they were catalogued. Its strongest achievement is to expose the structure of the archive: not simply strange lights over a region, but decades of witness accounts, press stories, investigations, suspected explanations and unresolved gaps.

What it cannot do is turn concentration into confirmation. A dense patch of markers does not by itself demonstrate unusual aircraft, extraterrestrial activity or even a genuine physical hotspot. It may reflect population, visibility, publicity, repeated coverage or the diligence of local collectors. The map becomes evidentially useful only when its geographical layer is combined with the quality of each underlying report.

Read in that way, it is less a map of unexplained craft than a map of Apulia’s recorded encounters with uncertainty. That distinction does not diminish the project. It is precisely what makes the archive valuable: it allows celebrated cases, weak reports, plausible misidentifications and genuinely unresolved observations to be compared within one regional historical record.
