## Introduction

Eyewitnesses can describe how quickly a light crossed their field of view, but they usually cannot determine its true speed unless its distance is known. That distinction is crucial in [Friuli-Venezia Giulia]({{ 'friuli-venezia-giulia/' | relative_url }}), where several UFO reports contain estimated heights, descriptions such as “rapid”, or impressions of sudden acceleration without published radar tracks or measured range. A light that seems to cover a large stretch of sky in seconds may be a nearby, modestly moving object—or a distant object travelling much farther and faster. The same apparent motion can fit radically different physical speeds.

This does not make witnesses dishonest or their sightings worthless. It means that a vivid visual impression is not automatically a measurement. ## Why range is hard to judge against an empty sky

Human vision estimates distance by combining several cues: the known size of familiar objects, overlap with the landscape, texture, perspective, binocular depth and changes produced as the observer moves. Many of those cues disappear when the target is only a point of light against darkness or cloud.

At ordinary outdoor distances, binocular vision becomes progressively less useful. A light high in the sky may therefore look much the same whether it is a small object hundreds of metres away or a large one many kilometres away. Brightness offers little help because the witness normally does not know the light’s real power, dimensions or orientation. Atmospheric haze can also alter apparent brightness and contrast.

Aviation guidance treats this as a practical safety issue rather than a theoretical curiosity. The US Federal Aviation Administration explains that night vision depends heavily on limited visual information, while the loss of normal external references makes judgements of distance, speed and depth less dependable. A separate FAA safety briefing describes autokinesis, in which a stationary light viewed against a dark, featureless background can appear to drift after sustained observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/Night_Ops_Ch13.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[faa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Chapter 13: Night Operations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation Administration Chapter 13: Night Operations</span></span></span>

This matters in Friuli because many regional observations occurred before dawn or at night and involved luminous, poorly resolved objects. In the official record for [Cordenons]({{ 'cordenons/' | relative_url }}) on 30 December 2010, private citizens described a flat white object towards the south-east at 05:55. The archive lists it as stationary and gives an altitude of “about 600 metres”. Yet the published entry supplies no method by which that height was obtained—no triangulation, radar range or photographic scale. The number therefore reads as an eyewitness estimate entered into the case table, not an independently demonstrated altitude.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/OVNI-2010.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

That distinction is easy to miss. A precise-looking figure such as 600 metres can sound more authoritative than “fairly low”, even when both expressions derive from the same unaided visual impression. Unless the report explains how the range was measured, the extra numerical detail does not necessarily improve the evidence.

## How distance errors inflate speed claims

A witness directly sees **angular motion**: the rate at which an object changes position across the visual field. True linear speed must then be calculated from angular motion and distance. In simplified form:

**speed ≈ angular rate × distance**<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.faasafety.gov/files/events/SO/SO15/2024/SO15134204/YourSensesInTheShadows.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faasafety.gov">[faasafety.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faasafety.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FAA Safety Your Senses in the Shadows</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nighttime Visual Illusions...By using visual references, the pilot can gather information about distance, speed, and depth. If the assumed distance is doubled, the calculated speed roughly doubles. If the object is actually ten times farther away than guessed, the inferred physical speed changes by about a factor of ten.

Imagine a light moving through five degrees of sky in one second. If it is only 200 metres away, its sideways motion would be roughly 17 metres per second, or about 61 kilometres per hour. At two kilometres, the same angular movement corresponds to roughly 175 metres per second, or about 630 kilometres per hour. At twenty kilometres, it implies around 6,300 kilometres per hour. The eye sees the same five-degree sweep in every version; the spectacular result appears only after a distance is selected.

The reverse error is equally important. A witness may assume that a bright object is distant and aircraft-sized when it is actually a much closer lantern, balloon, drone, insect or illuminated debris. The supposed journey across several kilometres then shrinks to movement across a much shorter path. Size, distance and speed are linked: when none is independently known, assigning a value to one can create misleading confidence in the others.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2018/11/ufo-identification-process/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skeptical Inquirer UFO Identification Process</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical InquirerUFO Identification ProcessNovember 1, 2018 — An important basic observation-related fact is that neither an estimate of...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2018</span></span></span>

Sudden “acceleration” can also be an effect of geometry. An object approaching along the observer’s line of sight may appear nearly stationary and then begin crossing the sky rapidly as its path turns sideways. A nearby object passing the witness produces a much greater angular rate than the same object at long range, even if its actual speed does not change. Conversely, a distant aircraft can travel quickly while appearing to crawl because its angular displacement is small.

Changes in brightness may add another illusion. A light turning towards the observer can brighten without coming closer; a rotating aircraft, balloon payload or drone can expose or conceal lamps and reflective surfaces. When the object brightens, then shifts position or disappears, a witness may reasonably remember it as having approached, accelerated and shot away, although the observations do not uniquely establish that sequence.

## Apparent motion is not proof of exceptional performance

The danger is not confined to civilian UFO reports. Even trained observers using sophisticated equipment can misread apparent speed when range, sensor movement and viewing geometry are uncertain.

The US All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office has noted that forced perspective and parallax can produce exaggerated impressions of size and speed. Parallax occurs when the observer or camera moves, making a nearer object appear to sweep rapidly against a more distant background. Without an accurate three-dimensional reconstruction, some of the apparent motion attributed to the target may actually come from the observing platform.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Information%20Papers/AARO_Effect_of_Forced_Perspective_and_Parallax_View_on_UAP_Observations_2024.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Effect of Forced Perspective and Parallax View on UAP Observations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effect of Forced Perspective and Parallax View on UAP Observations...</span></span></span>

The military “GoFast” video provides a useful comparison because it contained far more information than a typical Friuli eyewitness account. Analysts had sensor angles, aircraft altitude, aircraft speed, frame timing and a displayed range to the target. Even so, missing information about the aircraft’s exact position and heading prevented a single unambiguous speed calculation. AARO modelled a range of scenarios and concluded that the striking visual impression of a low, exceptionally fast object was largely caused by motion parallax; its possible movements were compatible with ordinary wind-related behaviour rather than extraordinary propulsion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_GoFast_Case_Resolution_Card_Methodology_Final.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Go Fast Case Resolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO GoFast Case Resolution...</span></span></span>

That finding does not explain any particular Friuli sighting. It demonstrates a more limited and important point: if a sensor-rich military recording requires careful reconstruction before speed can be assessed, an unaided observation of a light with guessed altitude cannot support a stronger numerical claim.

## What instrumented measurements would require

A defensible speed estimate needs more than a witness saying that an object was “very fast”. Investigators must establish both how far it moved and how long the movement took, in real-world units rather than impressions.

The strongest reconstruction would normally require:

* accurately synchronised observation times;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* the witness or camera’s surveyed location;
* calibrated azimuth and elevation angles;
* reliable frame timing and camera field of view;
* independently measured range, or observations from two separated locations that allow triangulation;
* information about any movement of the observer, aircraft or camera;
* weather data, including winds at different altitudes;
* radar, transponder or other sensor records where available;
* original files with complete metadata rather than compressed copies or retellings.

</div>

Two separated viewing positions are especially valuable. Their different lines of sight can intersect to provide a three-dimensional location. Repeating that calculation at successive times yields a trajectory and velocity. NASA’s independent UAP study recommended multiple well-calibrated sensors and noted that near-simultaneous observations with reliable location and timing metadata could be used to triangulate an object and estimate its velocity and size.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Science...</span></span></span>

Radar can be important, but “radar confirmation” must itself be examined carefully. Investigators need the original track, range, altitude, timing, sensor limitations and confidence that the return corresponds to the visually observed object. A statement that officials checked known flying activity is not equivalent to publication of a correlated radar plot.

This distinction applies directly to the Italian Air Force’s Friuli records. The Cordenons entry says that available checks could not associate the event with known flight or radiosonde activity. That preserves the case as unidentified within the available record, but it does not turn the estimated 600-metre altitude into a measured value or establish unusual speed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/OVNI-2010.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

## How to read Friuli speed claims fairly

The most responsible approach is neither to dismiss witnesses nor to convert their impressions into aeronautical performance figures. A report can be sincere, unusual and unresolved while still providing no reliable evidence of extreme velocity.

Descriptions such as “faster than an aircraft”, “shot away” or “crossed the sky instantly” are useful testimony about appearance. They may help investigators compare reports, reconstruct where witnesses were looking or identify a shared stimulus. They become problematic only when repeated as though they were measured speeds.

For Friuli-Venezia Giulia’s UFO history, three questions should accompany every dramatic movement claim:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Was distance independently established?** An estimated altitude based solely on appearance is not enough.
2. **Was the duration timed or remembered afterwards?** A difference of a few seconds can greatly alter the result.
3. **Was the observation corroborated by calibrated equipment or another separated witness position?** Multiple people standing together share almost the same uncertain line of sight and do not automatically provide triangulation.

</div>

Where those conditions are absent, the safest conclusion is not that the object moved slowly, nor that nothing unusual occurred. It is that its true speed is unknown. That may sound less exciting than an estimate of thousands of kilometres per hour, but it is a more accurate account of what the surviving Friuli evidence can actually support.
