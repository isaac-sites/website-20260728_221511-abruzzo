## Introduction

The Italian Air Force’s official files establish that UFO reports from [Marche]({{ 'how-marches-ufo-legends-took-shape/' | relative_url }}) were received, recorded and checked within a national aviation-security system. They do not establish that extraterrestrial craft visited the region. In official usage, an object remained “unidentified” when the available information could not be matched confidently to aircraft activity, weather, astronomy or another known cause.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Marche_113fca_air_force_official_r_dd3dd9-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Official Records" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That distinction matters because the Air Force archive gives several Marche sightings an official status that can easily be overstated. Entries from Tolentino, Ancona, Pesaro and the Adriatic coast confirm that reports reached military channels and survived in summary form. Yet most published records contain no radar plots, photographs, full witness statements or detailed reasoning. The archive is therefore valuable as a chronology of reported events and institutional handling, but much weaker as proof of what witnesses actually saw.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/ovni/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aeronautica Militare OVNI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica MilitareOVNI - Aeronautica Militare...</span></span></span>

## Why the Air Force took responsibility in 1978

Italy’s present system grew out of the exceptional national sighting wave of 1978. The Air Force states that Prime Minister Giulio Andreotti designated it as the institution responsible for collecting, checking and monitoring reports of unidentified flying objects. The work is now handled by the General Security Department of the Air Staff.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/ovni/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aeronautica Militare OVNI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica MilitareOVNI - Aeronautica Militare...</span></span></span>

The official objective is practical rather than speculative: protecting flight safety and national security. A sighting may indicate an unrecognised aircraft, unusual aviation activity, a meteorological event, an astronomical object or something else that could matter to airspace management. The Air Force does not describe its role as deciding whether intelligent extraterrestrial life exists. A former head of the responsible department stated plainly that this question falls outside its institutional competence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.media.inaf.it/2015/09/14/massimo-berti-intervista/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.inaf.it">[MEDIA INAF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.inaf.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MEDIA INAFMassimo Berti, il generale degli UFO – MEDIA INAF</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MEDIA INAFMassimo Berti, il generale degli UFO – MEDIA INAF</span></span></span>

The Carabinieri may add locally relevant information before the documentation is sent to the Air Force. Technical bodies concerned with air traffic, air defence and meteorology can then be asked to look for correlations, including possible matches in radar or operational records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/ovni/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aeronautica Militare OVNI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica MilitareOVNI - Aeronautica Militare...</span></span></span>

This procedure helps explain why official and civilian UFO catalogues do not contain the same cases. Many unusual lights reported to journalists, local investigators or websites may never have been submitted through the prescribed channel. Conversely, some military entries may have attracted little public attention. The official archive is therefore not a complete census of everything reported over Marche.

## What the Marche records actually contain

The Air Force’s historical publication is organised as a series of compact case tables. These normally give a location, date, time, reported appearance, motion, estimated height, weather conditions and type of witness. A short finding then records whether investigators identified a correlation.

Several entries relate directly to Marche.

**Tolentino, 22 December 1974.** A private citizen reported an ovoid object with white and orange lights, seen at about 00:30 and again at 03:30. The object was said to move from north-west to south-east at an estimated height of roughly 300 metres. The surviving summary says that examination of the archived information led to the event being catalogued as unidentified. It does not publish the witness’s complete account, the duration of either observation or any independent confirmation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Archivio_OVNI_periodo1972-1990.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

**Ancona and other Italian locations, 9 March 1978.** Air Force personnel and civilian pilots reported an elongated red-and-green phenomenon between approximately 20:30 and 20:40. The archive groups Ancona with Terni, Bologna, the Gran Sasso area and Vicenza, suggesting that observers in widely separated locations may have been watching the same high-altitude or astronomical event. Nevertheless, the official summary records no identified correlation and retains the case as an unidentified object report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Archivio_OVNI_periodo1972-1990.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

This is an important example of why “unidentified” should not be read as “unexplainable in principle”. A phenomenon visible over several distant parts of Italy is unlikely to have been a small object flying close to each observer. A bright meteor, re-entering space debris or another distant luminous event would fit the geographical pattern better than a local craft over Ancona. The published table, however, contains too little timing, trajectory or observational detail to test those possibilities decisively.

**The Adriatic coast, 4 November 1986.** Private citizens from a coastal area extending through Forlì, Pesaro and Ancona reported a half-moon-shaped object or light, coloured red and blue and seen towards the west at an elevation of about 20 to 30 degrees. Haze was noted. The Air Force summary again states only that the archived data were examined and the incident was catalogued as unidentified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Archivio_OVNI_periodo1972-1990.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

The broad coastal distribution makes this case more useful as evidence of a shared sighting than as evidence of a structured vehicle. A bright astronomical object distorted by haze, an aircraft viewed from different locations or a distant atmospheric display could produce broadly similar descriptions. The official entry does not disclose whether investigators compared individual bearings, checked aircraft routes or reconstructed the apparent position from each town.

**Tolentino, 21 April 1990.** A citizen reported a round green luminous source with a trail, travelling rapidly from south to north at an estimated altitude of 5,000 metres. The Air Force classified it as unidentified after examining the information in its files. The combination of very high speed, green light and a visible trail resembles the familiar appearance of a bright meteor, although the public summary does not record such an identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Archivio_OVNI_periodo1972-1990.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

A report from Rome about fourteen minutes later described a blue luminous source with a trail moving horizontally. The archive treats the reports separately, but their timing raises the possibility that observers in different regions saw a related astronomical or re-entry event. That remains an inference rather than an official conclusion because the published material does not provide enough trajectory data for a reliable reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Archivio_OVNI_periodo1972-1990.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

**Pesaro, 28 June 1990.** Several citizens reportedly observed a globular red-and-violet phenomenon moving from north to south at about 04:00 under clear skies. No altitude was given. The entry is potentially significant because aviation professionals were involved, but the public table supplies no aircraft identification, exact position, distance, duration, radar confirmation or description of how many objects were observed. It therefore preserves a potentially interesting pilot case without providing enough evidence to judge its strength.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Archivio_OVNI_periodo1972-1990.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

## How a report remains unidentified

The Air Force’s definition is procedural. Investigators seek correlations with known human activity or natural phenomena. When the available evidence allows a firm match, the event can be identified. When it does not, the record remains an unidentified flying object report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/ovni/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aeronautica Militare OVNI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica MilitareOVNI - Aeronautica Militare...</span></span></span>

This system contains an unavoidable evidence problem: failure to identify a cause may reflect lack of information rather than unusual behaviour. A report is especially likely to remain unresolved when it arrives late or lacks:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a precise observer position;
* accurate start and end times;
* angular measurements or compass bearings;
* photographs with reliable metadata;
* radar or air-traffic records;
* independent witnesses interviewed separately;
* astronomical and satellite comparisons made for the correct time.

</div>

The former head of the Air Force department explained that its work concentrates on objectively checkable technical data and does not normally involve field interviews. That limits what the system can establish in cases where the main evidence is human memory. It also helps explain why many reports remain unidentified: the investigators may be able to rule out an obvious scheduled flight without possessing enough evidence to identify a distant light positively.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.media.inaf.it/2015/09/14/massimo-berti-intervista/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.inaf.it">[MEDIA INAF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.inaf.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MEDIA INAFMassimo Berti, il generale degli UFO – MEDIA INAF</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MEDIA INAFMassimo Berti, il generale degli UFO – MEDIA INAF</span></span></span>

The archive itself demonstrates this difference. Some Italian entries contain a specific conventional conclusion, such as a bright astronomical body or a civil aircraft approaching an airport. Many others use a standard statement that no correlation emerged from the material examined and that the event was therefore catalogued as unidentified. The latter wording records the outcome of a search, not a finding that normal explanations were impossible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Archivio_OVNI_periodo1972-1990.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

## Why “official” does not mean confirmed

Military handling strengthens one part of a report: it shows that a notification entered an institutional process rather than existing only as hearsay. It may also show that air-traffic, meteorological or defence bodies were consulted. This is particularly useful for dating incidents and distinguishing genuine contemporary reports from stories reconstructed decades later.

It does not automatically strengthen the underlying extraordinary claim. An official file can accurately record an inaccurate distance estimate, a mistaken description or a sincere misidentification. The Air Force generally publishes compressed summaries rather than the entire evidential package, so readers often cannot see how firmly a reported shape, altitude or speed was established.

Three labels should therefore be kept separate:

**Reported:** someone submitted an account through an official or military channel.

**Unidentified:** the available investigation did not establish a sufficiently certain cause.

**Extraordinary:** the evidence demonstrates performance or characteristics that cannot reasonably be attributed to conventional objects or natural events.

The Marche archive clearly supports the first category and contains a number of cases in the second. The published records do not, by themselves, establish the third.

Pilot or military witnesses deserve careful attention because they may understand aircraft behaviour and aviation conditions better than the average observer. Even so, occupational expertise does not provide precise range, scale or speed when an unfamiliar light lacks a known distance. The Ancona–Bolzano crew report is therefore more notable than an anonymous internet claim, but it is not decisive without the missing flight, positional and sensor data.

## Missing files and the limits of public access

The Air Force’s decision to publish historical and annual records is valuable. It enables readers to verify that named locations and dates really appear in an official archive, and it discourages claims that every military UFO report was simply suppressed. Researchers and journalists have also been given access to fuller folders, some of which contained testimony and drawings not reproduced in the online tables.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.analisidifesa.it/2014/03/ufo-i-dossier-italiani/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: analisidifesa.it">[analisidifesa.it]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">analisidifesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UF O – I dossier italiani – Analisi Difesa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UF O – I dossier italiani – Analisi Difesa</span></span></span>

However, public access is uneven. The web archive is largely a database of conclusions and extracted fields, not a complete release of every original document. For many Marche cases, the reader cannot inspect:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* the signed report form;
* the Carabinieri covering note;
* witness sketches or photographs;
* radar-search results;
* meteorological consultations;
* aviation checks;
* correspondence between offices;
* the reasoning behind the final classification.

</div>

This absence does not prove that records were destroyed or concealed. Some information may remain in administrative files, may never have been collected or may have been unavailable by the time the report was examined. It does mean that independent researchers cannot fully reproduce most historical assessments from the online summaries alone.

The period covered also creates confusion. The national archive includes reports dating from before the formal 1978 assignment, including the 1974 Tolentino case. This indicates that the Air Force possessed or later incorporated earlier records, but it should not be taken to mean that the post-1978 reporting structure operated unchanged throughout the preceding years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aeronautica.difesa.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Archivio_OVNI_periodo1972-1990.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aeronautica.difesa.it">[Aeronautica Militare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aeronautica.difesa.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aeronautica Militare</span></span></span>

The published total of 21 Marche cases should likewise be treated as an archival count, not a complete regional total. Civilian databases list many more Marche sightings because they accept press reports, private investigations and historical anecdotes that were never filed officially. The gap between the two systems is evidence of different collection methods, not proof that one side is necessarily hiding or inventing cases.

## The most reliable reading of the archive

The Italian Air Force records make three defensible contributions to Marche’s UFO history.

First, they confirm that reports from the region entered a national security and flight-safety process. Tolentino, Pesaro, Ancona and the Adriatic corridor were not added to the story solely through later folklore.

Second, they preserve useful patterns. Several reports involved luminous objects seen across wide areas, coastal observation lines or civil aviation routes. These patterns can guide comparisons with meteors, space re-entries, aircraft movements and atmospheric conditions.

Third, the files impose limits on stronger claims. Their frequent use of an unidentified classification shows that no firm cause was established from the available evidence. It does not show that investigators detected alien technology, impossible flight performance or a threat to national airspace.

For Marche, the official archive is therefore best regarded as a disciplined starting point rather than a final verdict. It is stronger than unsupported retelling because it preserves dates, places, witness categories and an institutional outcome. It is weaker than a full technical investigation because much of the underlying evidence and reasoning is absent from public view. The unresolved cases remain legitimate subjects for historical inquiry, but their official status should not be made to carry more weight than the records themselves can support.
